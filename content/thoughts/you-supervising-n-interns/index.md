Title: You, Supervising N Interns 
Date: 2026-11-18 09:00 
Modified: 2026-11-20 12:30 
Category: Thoughts 
Tags: AI, agents, management, education, work

Authors: Robert Brunner 
Summary: Moving from chat to agents hands the intermediate decisions to the machine. The job that remains is the one you learned supervising interns. Start with small tasks, check the work, and own the result.

{% newthought "I was recently describing" %} to a client how AI work has changed when I wondered just how we got here.

A year ago, we sat with a prompt. We asked, it answered, and every decision in between was ours, including the bad ones.

Now we hand it an assignment and off it goes. Which source to trust, which exception to skip, which shortcut is harmless: it decides, and we find out later. We still own the result. But we no longer make every choice that produces it.

On the face of it, this is unsettling, and it should be. Our reputation matters; have we outsourced it?

Yet this is also a job most of us have done before, which is what I told the client. It is not just you anymore. It is you, supervising N interns.{% sidenote "faculty-piece" "A gentler version of this metaphor, the skilled intern that drafts and summarizes under direction, appeared in [Dancing in the Dark](/posts/dancing-in-the-dark/), written for faculty in July 2025. This piece carries the harder version." %}

Most of us know this arrangement from one side or the other. Maybe you've had an intern, or been one, or watched a team absorb one. They arrive with a CV and no idea how your company or team works. Some are quick and some are not. What they share is that the work you put in at the start, the small tests, the explanations, and the checks, determines what you get out when the role expires.

But now, the AI version is the intern every manager was sold and never got. It is fast, fluent, tireless, and available in quantity. It has read everything but experienced nothing. It knows every pattern in the textbook but none of the consequences in your building.

A novice can now vibe-code a working application that is itself an agent.{% sidenote "ng" "Andrew Ng made the developer version of this point in August 2026: a novice can vibe-code a working app while the agent makes tradeoffs the novice did not know existed. The fundamentals he says the developer must still supply (data, the full stack, architecture, security and reliability, production) are the ones the supervisor has to supply for any agent." %}
And this new agent, unprompted, makes tradeoffs the novice did not know existed. The app runs. The decisions sit inside it, and the person who built it cannot see the choices being made, may not appreciate their implication, or even understand their impact.

**The machine took the typing. It did not take the knowing.**

This new world may seem scary. As David Byrne sings, "Well... how did I get here?"{% marginnote "mn-1" "Talking Heads, 'Once in a Lifetime,' from *Remain in Light* (1980), written by David Byrne, Brian Eno, and the band. [Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5IsSpAOD6K8)." %}

Thinking it through afterward, I understood why what I told the client had worked. The blueprint was the framework I use with a new student who joins my research team. I assign a small task, examine the result, and broaden the task when the result and the demonstrated understanding earn it. Nobody learns it from a textbook. It comes from trial and error, or from the experience of being on the other side. In the end, anyone who has supervised interns arrives at it, because it is the only thing that works.

If you've used agents (or good interns) the benefit is plain. You get more done. Yet the cost is that the job changes underneath you, which can be unsettling.

Someone else is making the intermediate choices, and the work that remains is judging what came back and owning it, whether or not you saw it being made. That is a real adaptation, and it is also one most people have already made once. Guiding an intern or a student teaches exactly these skills. You decide what to hand over, you read the output, and you own the result. The agent asks for nothing new in kind. It asks for those skills at higher volume, and the volume is the hard part. We are humans after all.

Change is hard; I am not saying otherwise. The intern mindset doesn't guarantee job security. Instead, it shows the path to becoming more valuable to the company. The person who can guide agents is in a better position than the person who refuses to do so. And refusing is not a neutral choice; the work is moving whether or not any particular employee moves with it.

Work with enough interns and you invariably get one that is afraid to ask questions or happily marches down blind alleys. While an agent isn't afraid, it is happy to guess, often fluently, and rarely admits it.{% sidenote "no-consequence" "There is a structural reason for this. The agent never faces a consequence for being wrong. It cannot be embarrassed, passed over, or let go. Whoever deployed it can, which is why the judgment has to sit with a human." %} I learned to ask students questions to get them to open up and get back on track. This same approach is needed with agents. Question what they do and why, or the trust is never earned.

An agent may seem to know it all, but commercial models are ignorant of your internal data, policies, and procedures. It does not know what your data contains, why a column went null in 2019, or which of your systems breaks when another one changes. It will build you a working demo, and the demo is not the deployment, and in a regulated business that gap is the audit. Production is three in the morning, the batch job, the failover, and the person on call.

Now, most of that is probably not reliably known by the humans either. The difference is that a person who has been in the building knows the question exists, knows who to ask, and knows when an answer is too good to be true. The agent misses all of that, and, more importantly, it will not realize the gap. That local knowledge is what the supervisor brings. An agent can only be steered around the obstacles it is shown, and nobody else in the room knows where they are.

Not everyone can do this right now, and that is the normal condition, not a verdict. Supervising an agent, like supervising a person, is a skill. This skill can be taught, which makes it an education problem before it is a management problem. I say that as a professor, and I mean it as an obligation. Judgment and verification are two skills a degree is supposed to ensure, and business schools that teach how to prompt but not how to supervise have failed the task. And for those already in the workplace, continuing education and corporate training must provide the same. The alternative reading, that the agents arrived and the people are on notice, is wrong. The people stay, and the job that stays with them is one that can be taught.

Supervising, then, is two jobs: telling the intern what to do, and judging what it did. The second is harder, because it does not scale the way the first does. Every manager knows there is a limit to how many people one person can supervise well, and N interns quickly demonstrates one's limits. So the judging spreads out, and it starts at the bottom. When one person has an agent, that person reads the output.

There is a next step, and many of the firms I talk to have barely started on it. Once everyone has agents, managing a person means managing someone who is managing agents. The team lead cannot read every output, so the lead is no longer reviewing a report. The lead is reviewing how a report got made. Who made it, where the agents sit, what they were allowed to touch, what was checked, and what was trusted. That is a management skill few have practiced, and it is why supervision does not stop at the individual.

One level up, the director is not reading workflows. The director is deciding which people are trusted to run them.

This is the same problem, at three different altitudes. The contributor judges the output, the lead judges the process, the director judges the people. None of these is a new job. The org chart does not have to change. What moves is the work inside each box, and the thing that moves into every box is judgment.

I gave this frame to a room of insurance executives this fall, and later to my dean, and in both cases the reaction was the same. Nobody required an explanation. They had all stood on every rung of that ladder. What they took from it was not the metaphor itself but what it did to the adoption problem. The executives' problem was never that AI might replace people. It was that people were afraid to use it, and the fear was reasonable, because nobody had told them what their job was once the typing was gone. The intern frame answers that. It gives people a role they already recognize, it puts their value in what the machine cannot supply, and it makes the accountability explicit, so that using the agent reads as management rather than surrender. It also sets the entry test. A workflow that cannot absorb a new human is not ready for an artificial one.{% sidenote "mechanics" "The mechanics follow once a firm has the frame. If the agent is an intern, its output is the organization's output, the same way an intern's work ships under the firm's name. Review is part of the job, so time spent checking is not a deduction from productivity, and usage is not a credit to it; nobody gets points for the number of agents they ran. And a workflow is not ready until someone can stop it when the agent is wrong and the metrics do not punish them for doing so." %}

The frame holds until someone raises the apprenticeship objection. After all, if the agent does the junior work, where does the next senior come from?{% sidenote "ghosts" "I raised the junior-hire version of this question in [Ghosts in the Machine](https://innodative.com/posts/ghosts-in-the-machine/) in July 2025, before agents made it urgent." %} The intern frame has to answer this or it is just a nice way of hiding attrition.

The answer is the rule I give my students: do not offload, or you risk being offloaded. The point is not discipline. It is that offloading is only possible for someone who already has the thing being offloaded. The supervisor in the earlier sections can judge the agent's output because they once produced that output themselves, slowly, perhaps badly, with someone looking over their shoulder. The knowing was built by doing the work that the agent now does. Skip the doing and there is no knowing with which to supervise, and the person who skipped it is the one for whom the frame has no role.

So the apprenticeship question has a real answer, and it is uncomfortable for firms in a hurry. The junior work is not the thing to automate first. It is the training set. A firm that automates it owes the next cohort another route to the same judgment, and most have not built one. That other route defines how a business school should frame its curriculum in the age of AI. A course that teaches the basics of AI is the start, not the answer. The answer is AI embedded as an intern throughout the rest of the coursework, with some of the work still done unaided, because the student has to build the knowing before there is anything to offload. Audit is taught as audit with an AI intern, marketing as marketing with one, operations as operations with one, and every assignment asks what the student checked and what they trusted. {% sidenote "judgment" "What that route looks like, from a question a technology manager asked me after a keynote this fall: (1) have people verify AI output against known answers and score what they catch; (2) have them write the spec before the machine generates anything; (3) route real exceptions to early-career staff with a senior reviewing their calls; (4) protect some reps with no AI at all. And measure reviewer accuracy, never throughput." %} The firm that hands all of it to agents has an efficient year and an empty bench, and the empty bench shows up at exactly the moment the firm needs someone who can tell if the agent is wrong.

Which brings us back to the song, "Same as it ever was. Same as it ever was."

The internship frame does not make the transition painless. It makes it legible. It tells people what their job is, it tells managers what they are managing, and it tells firms which work they cannot afford to automate. The agents are not going to ask for any of that. The asking, as always, is left to us.
