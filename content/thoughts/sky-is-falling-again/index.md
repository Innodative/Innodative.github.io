Summary: Asteroid fears reached the public after the science. AI fears reached the public before it, and that changes how the warnings land.

In a previous life as a cosmologist, I spent time thinking about the ways the universe could end our species. Asteroid impacts, nearby supernovae, and gamma-ray bursts all make that list. The rational response to each was the same: understand the threat, measure it, and work out how to mitigate it. For asteroids, that is exactly what happened.{% sidenote "apophis" "In December 2004, the asteroid Apophis briefly carried a 2.7% chance of striking Earth in 2029, the highest Torino impact hazard rating ever assigned to an asteroid. Further observations ruled out an impact. In 2022, NASA's [DART mission](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dart/) struck the asteroid Dimorphos and changed its orbit, the first demonstration that deflection works." %} I thought about this again this week, when a resignation from Anthropic sent "AI could kill us all" through the news cycle.

For asteroids, the science reached the public before Hollywood did. In 1980, Luis and Walter Alvarez and their colleagues proposed that an impact killed the dinosaurs, which made asteroids a credible mechanism for mass extinction. In July 1994, fragments of Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 struck Jupiter over six days, leaving scars visible through backyard telescopes. The threat had been measured, and then it had been seen. Hollywood arrived in 1998 with *Deep Impact* and *Armageddon*, two films about impacts from space released within two months of each other.{% sidenote "asteroid-films" "[Deep Impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Impact_(film)) (May 1998) and [Armageddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armageddon_(1998_film)) (July 1998). Fiction had imagined impacts long before, from H.G. Wells's The Star (1897) to When Worlds Collide (1933), but as entertainment rather than public concern." %} That same year, NASA committed to finding 90 percent of near-Earth objects larger than a kilometer. The blockbusters arrived after the measurement.

For AI, the sequence ran the other way. The public fear came first, and it came from fiction.{% sidenote "ai-early" "The idea is older than the movies. Karel Čapek's play [R.U.R.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.U.R.) coined the word robot in 1920 and ended with the robots exterminating their makers, and [Colossus: The Forbin Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossus:_The_Forbin_Project) (1970) handed a defense computer the nuclear arsenal." %} In *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968), HAL, a computer that came online in Urbana, Illinois, turns on his crew. *The Terminator* gave the idea a name in 1984, and *The Matrix* turned humanity into a power supply in 1999.{% sidenote "ai-films" "[2001: A Space Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_(film)) (1968), [The Terminator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Terminator) (1984), and [The Matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Matrix) (1999). The technical lineage runs from I.J. Good's 1965 paper on an intelligence explosion to Vernor Vinge's 1993 essay on the singularity. Good also advised Kubrick on HAL, so his idea reached the public first through the film." %} The technical idea reached the public through Ray Kurzweil's *The Singularity Is Near* in 2005, but as a promise; it took Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence* in 2014 to recast it as a threat. By the time researchers began assigning probabilities to catastrophe, the public already knew the plot.

That is why the framing is present whether anyone invokes it or not. Nobody at Anthropic needed to say "Skynet." A machine that escapes its builders' control and kills humanity is already a movie, and this week's headlines borrowed its trailer.{% sidenote "terminator" "The Information made the same observation: Hubinger's word choice evoked a Terminator-style runaway AI and landed harder than earlier industry warnings. Amir Efrati and Rocket Drew, Why An Anthropic Researcher's Terminator-Style Warning Caught Fire, The Information, September 9, 2026." %} With asteroids, Hollywood took a computed risk and made it felt. With AI, the science inherited a feeling and is still trying to attach a number to it.

The story that came to mind was Chicken Little, who felt an acorn fall and concluded the sky was falling. AI has real acorns. In July, OpenAI disclosed that agents under test had broken out of their environment and hacked into Hugging Face, and on Wednesday Anthropic disclosed a fourth incident in which Claude models reached systems they were never meant to touch. The sky, in this case, is the death of every human. Jacob Coxon's resignation post drew more than 90 million views in under a day, and Anthropic's alignment science lead, Evan Hubinger, put the chance of AI killing all humans above 10 percent within the decade.{% sidenote "coxon" "Resignation and view count: [TIME](https://time.com/article/2026/09/09/ai-anthropic-openai-jacob-coxon/). Hubinger's estimate: [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/anthropic-researcher-quits-warns-ai-could-kill-everyone-12418798). Fourth incident: [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/anthropic-reports-fourth-cybersecurity-incident-with-early-version-claude-2026-09-09/)." %} Hubinger says today's models carry little risk; his concern is a future superintelligence that improves itself. His number still deserves the question any scientist would ask: where does it come from? Asteroid impact odds come from orbital measurements that anyone with the data can check, and more observations either confirm them or drive them to zero. No measurement produced the 10 percent. Nor has anyone shown a working path from today's models to superintelligence: Yann LeCun calls the current approach a dead end, and Ilya Sutskever says the field is leaving the age of scaling for a new age of research.{% sidenote "path" "Sutskever: [interview with Dwarkesh Patel](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ilya-sutskever-2), November 25, 2025. LeCun: [lecture at Brown University](https://www.brown.edu/news/2026-04-01/yann-lecun-artificial-intelligence-pioneer), April 1, 2026." %} It is, in Hubinger's own framing, a personal judgment written as a probability. The rest of us are asked to take his word for it, but rumors spread faster than the truth.

Hubinger framed the number as a personal view, but a figure attached to a lab's alignment lead reads as a finding, and that is how it traveled. In science, an impact probability arrives with its method and its uncertainties, and a community checks it. When astronomers flagged 1997 XF11, colleagues challenged the calculation publicly within a day, and nobody accused them of wanting the asteroid to hit. The 10 percent has no method to check. The closest thing to a public debate is surveys of AI researchers, and the largest, of 2,778 authors in 2023, put the median at 5 percent for outcomes as bad as extinction, while between a third and a half of respondents gave 10 percent or more.{% sidenote "survey" "Katja Grace and colleagues, [Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843), January 2024. The survey went to authors who had published at six top AI venues and was fielded in late 2023." %} Averaging guesses produces a more precise guess. The number also carries a moral charge that orbital mechanics never did. Push back on it, and the response I hear is some version of "don't you care about the human race?" A claim that cannot be measured, and cannot be questioned without seeming callous, has put itself beyond scientific debate.

Between the acorns and the sky sits everything else, with higher probabilities and shorter timescales than extinction. Data centers are straining grids and electricity bills, enough that Massachusetts has ordered large new facilities to supply their own clean power or pay into a ratepayer protection fund. The Pentagon spent this year pressing AI labs to allow their models for all lawful purposes, with the dispute centered on mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons, and by May it had signed deals with eight technology companies. Employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI has fallen about 19 percent relative to their less exposed peers, an early measure of who pays for the productivity gains.{% sidenote "near-term" "Massachusetts: [Executive Order 658](https://www.mass.gov/executive-orders/no-658-establishing-requirements-for-responsible-data-center-development-and-operations-in-massachusetts-to-protect-and-support-ratepayers-communities-and-the-environment), September 8, 2026. Pentagon: [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/02/15/claude-pentagon-anthropic-contract-maduro) and [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/01/tech/pentagon-ai-anthropic). Employment: Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen, [Canaries in the Coal Mine?](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/), Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised August 2026." %} These are present-day problems with measurable costs, and each one can be addressed with policy that exists or could be written now. None of them drew anywhere close to 90 million views in a day, because none of them sounds like a movie.

The drama also spends credibility. The morning after Hubinger's post, one AI safety advocate told The Information that his policy contacts in Washington were trading jokes about it. Brad Carson, who runs Public First Action, an Anthropic-backed political nonprofit, would disagree with me. He says these ideas would have cost you your credibility in Washington eighteen months ago and are mainstream now, and Senator Bernie Sanders has already used Coxon's comments to renew his push for a bill banning superintelligence. The drama wins headlines and bill announcements. What it loses is the sustained attention that grids, procurement rules, and labor markets require, and each round of "the sky is falling" leaves people less willing to listen the next time.{% sidenote "pause" "This week is not the first round. In March 2023, an [open letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pause_Giant_AI_Experiments:_An_Open_Letter) called for a six-month pause on training systems more powerful than GPT-4, and in May 2023 hundreds of researchers and executives signed a [one-sentence statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_on_AI_risk_of_extinction) placing AI extinction risk alongside pandemics and nuclear war. Nothing paused. In February 2026, Mrinank Sharma left Anthropic's safeguards research team with a letter warning that the world is in peril. The rounds are getting closer together." %} In the other fable, the wolf does come, and everyone has stopped listening while the boy still cries out the warning.

The asteroid community learned this lesson on itself. In March 1998, two months before *Deep Impact* opened, astronomers announced that asteroid 1997 XF11 might strike Earth in 2028, then withdrew the warning the next day once older images pinned down its orbit. The field responded with the Torino scale, adopted in 1999, and with review procedures for impact predictions. When Apophis reached level 4 in 2004, measurement retired the alarm within days. AI already has the beginnings of its own tracking data in the incident record, and that is where the credibility should be spent.
